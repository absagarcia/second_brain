## Core framing

Internet eras framed as **Read** (web 1.0, static content) → **Write**
(web 2.0, corporate/platform networks like Facebook, Twitter) → **Own**
(web3, blockchain networks where users hold tokens/property rights
directly). The book's central claim: corporate networks concentrate
power/money and "tax" creativity and innovation once they capture a
market — a shift from "permissionless" to "permissioned" internet.

## Protocol networks vs. corporate networks vs. blockchain networks

- **Protocol networks** (email, the open web): permissionless, but
  historically starved of financial incentives to keep improving.
- **Corporate networks** (Facebook, Twitter/X): centrally controlled —
  "your only choice is to stay, or leave and start from scratch
  somewhere else"; you can export your data but you can't take your
  network/audience with you.
- **Blockchain networks**: claimed to combine the open/permissionless
  properties of protocol networks with the competitive/financial
  capabilities of corporate networks, via token incentives that fund
  ongoing development without requiring central corporate ownership.

## Other notable claims (as highlighted)

- **Composability**: software as "Lego bricks" — smaller pieces assembled
  into larger compositions; framed as a property blockchain networks
  enable more fully than traditional corporate platforms.
- **Token incentives as built-in marketing**: "tokens are self-marketing"
  — top blockchain networks are cited as having spent little on
  traditional advertising because users/holders do the promotion
  themselves ("show me the incentives and I will show you the outcome" —
  Charlie Munger, quoted).
- **Take rates / business model**: networks monetize via fees on
  activity (commerce, advertising); the book argues value/fees can accrue
  to community-controlled blockchains instead of company-controlled
  front-ends.
- **Classification debate**: the book argues blockchain networks should
  be classified as commodities (like gold), not securities (like stock) —
  presented as the author's position in an active regulatory debate, not
  settled law.
- **Applications sketched**: NFTs as general-purpose ownership containers
  (not just art); stablecoin payments (USDC) as faster/cheaper than bank
  wires; AI + blockchain combined as a "new economic covenant for
  creators" (attribution/payment for AI-derived content) — all framed as
  emerging/speculative applications, not established outcomes.
