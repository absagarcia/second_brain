# De cero a uno (Zero to One: Notes on Startups, or How to Build the Future)

Widely known to be by Peter Thiel (with Blake Masters, 2014) — same
caveat as the other book ingests: not stated in the raw file itself (a
Kindle highlights export, no metadata page), so this attribution is
recalled general knowledge, not source-traceable.

**Nature of this source**: personal Kindle highlights export — same
double-compression caveat as the other book pages in this wiki. Coverage
spans chapters 1-11 and 14, plus the conclusion, unevenly (some chapters
have a single highlight).

## Core thesis

"0 to 1" = creating something genuinely new (vertical/intensive progress,
the word for which is **technology**), as opposed to "1 to n" = copying
what already works (horizontal/extensive progress, i.e. globalization).

## Other recurring arguments (as highlighted)

- **Contrarian questions as method**: "identify a delirious popular
  belief, and you can find the paradoxical truth hiding behind it" — the
  book's central intellectual move, echoed later as the definition of a
  **secret**: "what valuable company is nobody building?" is necessarily
  a secret — something important, unknown, hard but feasible.
- **Startup dogma pushed back on (Ch. 2)**: Thiel explicitly argues
  against the dot-com-era lessons (make incremental progress; stay lean
  and flexible; improve on the competition; focus on product not sales)
  and instead claims: better to risk boldness than triviality; a bad plan
  is better than no plan; competitive markets destroy profits; sales
  matter as much as product, not more.
- **Success is not a lottery ticket (Ch. 6)**: "success is never
  accidental" / "victory awaits the one who has everything in order;
  people call it luck" — argues a well-defined plan is systematically
  undervalued in a world that treats the future as random. Directly
  comparable to [[unicorn-startup-status]]'s point that unicorn founders
  are often serial entrepreneurs, not one-off lottery winners.
- **Follow the money / power law (Ch. 7)**: most startups fail and most
  VC funds fail alongside them, per Thiel — venture returns follow a
  power law rather than a normal distribution, so a small number of
  outsized winners matter far more than portfolio diversification. A
  founder's own time is framed as their biggest investment in the
  company, ahead of any external capital.
- **Team/culture ("the mafia") and founders (Ch. 9-10)**: a problematic
  board member can jeopardize a company's future; "you're either on the
  bus or off the bus" (Ken Kesey) as a framing for all-in commitment; cash
  compensation is framed as oriented toward the present, not the future
  (implicitly favoring equity for long-term alignment); the question of
  why anyone would join as, e.g., "employee #20" instead of taking a
  higher-paid, higher-prestige job at an incumbent is posed as a real
  recruiting problem for challenger startups.
- **Sales matters (Ch. 11)**: "if you've invented something new but
  haven't invented an effective way to sell it, you have a bad business,
  regardless of how good the product is" — engineers/technical founders
  are warned against underestimating how much work it takes to make sales
  look effortless.
- **The Founder's Paradox (Ch. 14)**: only a passing mention of the "27
  Club" (musicians who died at 27) in the excerpt — presumably used as an
  analogy about the mythologizing of extreme founder personalities, but
  the highlighted text doesn't include enough surrounding context to
  state the actual argument here. Flagged as a gap, not summarized further.
- **Conclusion**: closes on "the first step is to think for yourself."

## Relevance to this wiki

The monopoly-vs-competition framework and the "success is never
accidental" argument are the most transferable pieces to
[[athletix-ai]]'s own competitive positioning — see
[[monopoly-vs-competition-zero-to-one]] for the cross-link. Also worth
noting a difference in emphasis (not a hard contradiction) with
[[creando-unicornios]]: Clip's founder is quoted there as "gana el que
ejecuta" (execution beats the idea), while Thiel's framing here puts more
weight on finding a genuine secret/monopoly position *before* execution
matters. Both books would likely agree both matter — this is a difference
in what each chooses to emphasize, not a documented disagreement between
them.
