# Imagined Orders / Shared Fictions as the Basis of Large-Scale Cooperation

**Long-lived framework**: Yuval Noah Harari's central mechanism in
[[sapiens]] for explaining large-scale human cooperation — an
anthropological/historical thesis, not tied to any dataset that decays.

## The core claim

Principles like human equality, human rights, nations, money, and
corporations have no objective existence outside "the fertile imagination
of sapiens" and the myths they tell each other — yet these "imagined
orders" aren't conspiracies or useless illusions. They're described as
*the only way* large numbers of humans can cooperate effectively at all,
since genuine large-scale cooperation requires convincing strangers (who
share no personal relationship) to act in coordinated ways, and that only
happens if they believe some of the same myths.

## Mechanics of how an imagined order sticks

1. **Never admit it's imagined** — societies insist the order reflects
   objective reality (gods, nature's laws), not a human invention.
2. **It shapes desires from birth** — people are born into a pre-existing
   imagined order and their personal wants are molded by its dominant
   myths from the start, so personal desire becomes the order's strongest
   defense (illustrated with "follow your heart" as itself a product of
   19th-century romantic myth and 20th-century consumerist myth, not a
   natural impulse).
3. **It's intersubjective, not subjective or objective** — an objective
   phenomenon exists independent of any consciousness (gravity); a
   subjective one exists only in one individual's mind and vanishes when
   they stop believing it (a child's imaginary friend); an intersubjective
   one (money, human rights, nations) exists in the shared communication
   network of many minds, and only unravels if a large fraction of that
   network simultaneously stops believing — which in practice requires
   another complex organization (a movement, party, or cult) to
   coordinate, which itself only works if people believe some shared myth
   to cooperate with strangers in the first place.

## Relevance to this wiki

A general framework for cross-project reflection on how large
organizations/movements (companies, standards bodies, communities) get
strangers to cooperate — potentially relevant background for
[[monopoly-vs-competition-zero-to-one]]'s point about branding as a
monopoly characteristic (brand is arguably a small-scale "imagined
order") and for [[network-effects-as-moat]]'s discussion of

not asserted as a documented link in either source, just a plausible
cross-reading.
