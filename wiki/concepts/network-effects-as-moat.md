# Network Effects as a Competitive Moat

**Long-lived framework, converging from three independent sources**: this
concept synthesizes matching claims from [[read-write-own]] (Chris
Dixon), [[de-cero-a-uno]] (Peter Thiel), and [[the-saas-playbook]] (Rob
Walling) — none of which cite each other, but which land on closely
related points about what makes a network defensible.

## The shared claim

- [[de-cero-a-uno]] lists network effects as one of four characteristics
  monopolies tend to share (alongside proprietary technology, economies
  of scale, and branding) — see [[monopoly-vs-competition-zero-to-one]].
- [[read-write-own]] treats network effects as *the* central mechanic of
  internet power: "on the internet, marginal costs of distribution are
  negligible, so power primarily accrues another way: through network
  effects." It further distinguishes *who captures* that effect — a
  corporate network (e.g. Facebook) captures it centrally and can strand
  users who can't take their audience elsewhere, whereas the book argues
  blockchain networks let the value accrue to the community/token holders
  instead of a single company's front-end.
- [[the-saas-playbook]] adds a specific caveat for bootstrapped SaaS
  companies: "the network effect moat comes not from users, but
  integrations" — i.e. small/bootstrapped companies often can't build a
  Facebook-style user-network moat, but can build a defensible position
  through deep product integrations instead.
- [[end-of-the-app-store]] (an earlier, unsourced/speculative page in
  this wiki, from a conversation with [[daniel]]) makes the same point
  from yet another angle: even if app *generation* becomes free via LLMs,
  audience/network moats (Airbnb, eBay, TikTok) survive because the value
  was never really in the software.

## What differs between the sources

- Thiel and the SaaS Playbook both treat network effects as one
  *defensive* tool among several (alongside proprietary tech, brand,
  integrations); Dixon treats it as closer to *the* defining mechanic of
  internet-scale power, with the added political argument that *who*
  captures the network's value (a company vs. a community of token
  holders) is itself the central design choice of web3.
- The SaaS Playbook's "integrations, not users" point is the most
  actionable/specific for smaller companies — a useful corrective to
  treating "network effects" as only meaning consumer-scale
  user-to-user effects.

## Relevance to this wiki

Directly relevant to [[athletix-ai]]'s own moat question: as a B2B SaaS
platform without a large consumer user-network, the
[[the-saas-playbook]] framing (integrations as the realistic moat for
smaller SaaS companies) is more applicable than Dixon's or Thiel's
consumer-network-scale framing — not asserted as something the team has
evaluated, just the most relevant lens among the three.
