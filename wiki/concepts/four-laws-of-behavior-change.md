# The Habit Loop, Four Laws of Behavior Change, and Identity-Based Habits

**Long-lived framework**: this is [[habitos-atomicos]]'s (James Clear)
central, still-current model for behavior change — not something that
decays like a data point.

## The habit loop

Every habit runs on a four-step feedback loop: **señal → anhelo →
respuesta → recompensa** (cue → craving → response → reward). A habit's
purpose is to solve a problem using the least energy/effort possible.

## The Four Laws of Behavior Change

Rules for engineering each step of the loop, per habit:

1. **Make it obvious** (cue) — self-awareness of your current cues/habits
   is the prerequisite; many performance failures trace back to lacking
   this awareness in the first place.
2. **Make it attractive** (craving) — the more attractive an opportunity,
   the more likely it forms a new habit. Uses **Premack's Principle**
   (David Premack): more probable behaviors reinforce less probable ones —
   basis for pairing a wanted-but-unlikely habit with an
   existing-and-likely one (habit stacking / temptation bundling).
3. **Make it easy** (response) — not detailed in this excerpt beyond the
   law's name (source highlights skip most of this section).
4. **Make it satisfying** (reward) — not detailed in this excerpt beyond
   the law's name.

**Implementation intentions** ("after [cue], I will [behavior]") are cited
as increasing adherence across habits as varied as recycling, studying,
sleeping earlier, and quitting smoking.

## Identity-based habits

Clear's distinction: outcomes are about what you get, processes are about
what you do, **identity is about what you believe**. The claim is that
durable habit change requires identity change first:

1. Decide what type of person you want to be.
2. Prove it to yourself through small, repeated wins.

Illustrated with the two-smokers example: refusing a cigarette by saying
"no thanks, I'm trying to quit" still holds a smoker's identity; saying
"no thanks, I'm not a smoker" reflects an actual identity shift. Also
illustrated with a friend who lost 50kg by repeatedly asking herself
"what would a healthy person do?" as a daily decision filter.

## Relevance to this wiki

Loosely comparable to [[habitos-para-ser-millonario]]'s "repeat to
yourself: I can do anything I set my mind to" self-talk habit — that's an
affirmation aimed at belief/confidence directly, whereas Clear's
identity-based habits work through repeated small actions that
accumulate into a belief, not the other way around. Not flagged as a hard
contradiction, just a different mechanism for a similar goal (self-belief
→ behavior, vs. behavior → self-belief).

[[deep-work]]'s "Rule #1: Work Deeply" independently makes the same
"make it easy" argument as this page's Second Law: fixed rituals (a set
time, a quiet location, a specific starting action) reduce the willpower
required to start deep-focus work, exactly as the Four Laws predict for
any habit.
